# Extensibility manifesto for OME-Zarr

“OME-Zarr must be extensible” –  at the recent hackathon held by the
Image Cooperative in Heidelberg (June 2026), this was an almost
unanimously agreed upon desirable characteristic for the upcoming
version 1.0 of the OME-Zarr format specification.

The aim of this document is to (hopefully) spell out clearly the
rationale behind this statement, what is actually meant by it, and to
define what an extensibility mechanism for OME-Zarr should look like.

## Scope
This document covers specifically OME-Zarr, that is, the OME-specific
parts of the OME-Zarr specification. It does not intend to address
extensibility at the level of the underlying Zarr format. The Zarr
community already has its own policies regarding extensibility, centered
around the concepts of “Zarr extensions” and “Zarr conventions”. This
document is not intended to supersede them.

## Rationale
We must acknowledge from the very beginning that, by its very nature,
OME-Zarr is already _de facto_ extensible, even before the word
“extensibility” makes it into the OME-Zarr specification.

Today, anyone can craft a OME-Zarr fileset that contains data beyond
what is described in the current version of the OME-Zarr specification.
Most if not all OME-Zarr reader implementations are inherently
“permissive”, in that they will silently ignore anything that they don’t
recognise or don’t expect within a OME-Zarr fileset, so that the
presence of additional data will typically not result in an unreadable
fileset.

We know for a fact that OME-Zarr filesets with additional data and/or
metadata do exist in the wild.

However, if left unchecked, this _de facto_ extensibility could quickly
become a barrier to interoperability. If every single creator of
OME-Zarr filesets can singlehandedly come up with its own way of
representing a particular piece of data, then there is a risk that a
particular piece of data may be understandable only by the very same
implementation that created it; or (arguably even worse) that a
particular piece of data may be understood _differently_ by different
implementations. Ultimately, OME-Zarr could end up as a file format that
is interoperable in name only: everybody would be using OME-Zarr, but
all in slightly incompatible ways.

The solution to that risk is not to “close down” OME-Zarr, to mandate
implementations to be non-permissive and to consider that anything that
is not explicitly allowed by the specification is forbidden. In fact,
even if we wanted to do that, we would have very little leverage to
actually enforce such a policy. So, OME-Zarr is _de facto_ extensible
and will **remain so**, whatever we do here.

Instead, we believe the proper solution is for OME-Zarr to provide a
built-in extensibility mechanism (which remains to be defined). There
would be no obligation for people willing to extend OME-Zarr to use such
a mechanism (again, we would have very little leverage to enforce such
an obligation anyway), but (voluntarily) using it would allow the
OME-Zarr community to manage the interoperability issues, which would
ultimately be for everyone’s benefit.

Another way to put it is that we want to promote a system of
_manageable_ and _interoperable_ extensibility, which would be preferred
over the _de facto_ extensibility that is already offered by the very
nature of OME-Zarr.

## Manageable extensibility: for who and what?
We do not wish to impose any arbitrary limit as to who can or should
extend OME-Zarr, or what they could extend it for. As stated in the
previous section, anyone who wishes to extend OME-Zarr can already do so
anyway, so any attempt to enforce restrictions would likely be
counterproductive (as it could push people away from the “manageable
extensibility” mechanism).

People who might want to extend OME-Zarr may include, for example,
equipment vendors (to store additional metadata generated by their
equipment), researchers (to store additional research results),
developers of analysis software (to store additional analysis data),
etc.

Importantly, this also includes the OME-Zarr “core team” (the authors of
the OME-Zarr specification) itself. Indeed, the “manageable
extensibility” we are envisioning here will be a way to keep OME-Zarr
evolving beyond the OME-Zarr 1.0 milestone, by allowing the core team to
bring new features to the format without ever introducing a breaking
change (a change that would require us to create a hypothetical
“OME-Zarr 2.0”), if the new features are introduced as “extensions”.

## Managing extensibility: how?
Any extensibility mechanism comes with risks for the system it is
opening to extensions, notably:

(a) the risk of a fragmented ecosystem, with an uncontrolled
proliferation of redundant and slightly incompatible extensions;

(b) the risk of uninterpretable extensions that cannot be understood
beyond their originating implementation.

Unmitigated, such risks could put into question the whole raison d’être
of OME-Zarr as a “standard” file format, because users of a given
OME-Zarr implementations may then not have a reasonable guarantee that
their OME-Zarr filesets could be fully exploited by users of other
implementations.

Those are not risks that can be mitigated at the level of the format
specification alone. Instead, the OME-Zarr community will need to take
steps to actively monitor the extension ecosystem and intervene as
needed to avoid the needless proliferation of redundant or concurrent
extensions. (In other words: the extensibility mechanism in OME-Zarr
will make extensibility manageable; but actually managing it will be the
job of the community.)

This would likely involve some sort of registry of known extensions,
which would have to be maintained by the community. Such a registry
would allow, for example, a putative extension creator to check whether
an extension similar to the one they were intent on creating already
exists. This would also allow one to detect when two similar extensions
are being created by independent groups, thereby giving the community a
chance to reach out to those groups to try and convince them to unite
their efforts behind a single extension instead.

## Extensions as a fundamental building block
Making extensibility manageable will require that extensions be treated
as first-class objects within the OME-Zarr specification.

In fact, we envision a design where every single feature of the OME-Zarr
format is fundamentally an “extension”.

In addition to making extensions a core aspect of the format, this would
allow “itemizing” the specification, which could then be treated as a
list of “extensions” (aka “features”) that happen to be considered
critical enough that all implementations must support them.

Under such a design, a given feature would be either a “core feature” or
an actual “extension” depending solely on whether it is listed in the
OME-Zarr specification. But there would be no fundamental, intrinsic
difference between a “core feature” and an “extension”.

Almost as a side effect, this would easily allow the production of
“feature matrixes”: implementers could generate machine-readable lists
of all the features (be they core features or actual extensions) that
their implementation supports, thereby allowing the community to get a
clear overview of which features are broadly supported at any given
moment.

## Desired characteristics for a “manageable extensibility” mechanism
(Those characteristics are not listed in any particular order.)

1.	Anyone can define an extension. Prior permission is not required.
2.	An extension can extend another extension, without arbitrary limits
    as to the “depth” of an extension chain.
3.	Data from an extension must be preserved by all implementations
    – including implementations that are not explicitly aware of that
    particular extension. OME-Zarr readers must be able to pass unknown
    extensions’ data down to their client code.
4.	Extensions must be unambiguously identifiable.
5.	An OME-Zarr reader must be able to quickly determine (and report to
    client code) the extensions that are being used within a OME-Zarr
    fileset, if any.
6.	OME-Zarr implementations must be able to unambiguously state which
    extensions they explicitly support, preferably in machine-readable
    form.
7.	Any extension must be clearly documented. The documentation must
    state what additional data the extension is intended to store, the
    purpose of such data, and where to find them.
8.	Any extension must provide a formal schema describing the shape of
    its data and allowing even an implementation that does not
    explicitly support the extension to perform basic validation.
9.	An extension should have a way to self-declare how “important” it is
    for a correct understanding of the OME-Zarr fileset in which it is
    used.
10.	The extensibility mechanism must ensure the absence of naming
    conflicts between two extensions developed independently.
11.	There must be a reasonably straightforward way to “promote” an
    extension so that it becomes part of the next version of the
    OME-Zarr specification, if there is agreement among the OME-Zarr
    community about the usefulness of the extension (how such an
    agreement could be reached and expressed is out of scope of this
    document, but presumably, the already existing RFC process could be
    reused).
12.	The promotion of an extension must not invalidate existing data
    created before the extension was promoted
